Senior Interview Playbook

How senior and staff backend loops are usually run and scored, how to drive an HLD or LLD round minute by minute, which questions come up and what each one really tests, where to steer the deep dive, and a six-week plan over this path. The 45-minute framework, estimation tables and core trade-offs are in System Design Foundations; this page assumes them.

How senior loops work

Part 1 of 8 · the rounds and the bar at each level

Loops differ by company and change over time, so treat this as the typical shape and ask your recruiter for the exact list of rounds before you prepare.

Typical loop composition

How senior loops work

A common senior backend loop is one or two screens followed by four to six onsite (or virtual onsite) rounds. Design carries more weight as the level rises: at staff, a weak design round is rarely offset by strong coding.

RoundTypical lengthWhat it assessesWeight at senior / staff
Coding (DSA)45 to 60 minProblem solving, clean correct code, complexity, testingGate: must pass; rarely decides the level
HLD (system design)45 to 60 minScoping, architecture, depth, trade-offs, scale and failureOften the main levelling signal; staff loops may have two
LLD / machine coding45 to 120 minObject modelling, patterns, extensibility, working code, concurrencyCommon for backend roles at many companies, especially product companies in India
Behavioural / leadership45 to 60 minOwnership, conflict, influence, mentoring, delivery under ambiguityHigh; at staff it often carries equal weight with design
Project or domain deep dive45 to 60 minReal depth on a system you built; judgement; honesty about trade-offsSometimes; more common at staff and for specialist roles
Hiring manager30 to 45 minTeam fit, motivation, scope you have ownedVaries; can include a light design or behavioural part

Interview tip: ask the recruiter three things: which rounds, whether LLD means "class diagram" or "running code", and the target level. Each changes what you practise.

Mid vs senior vs staff

How senior loops work

The same question is asked at every level; the bar moves. The difference is less about knowing more components and more about scope, initiative and judgement.

DimensionMid-levelSeniorStaff
ScopeDesigns the boxes asked forDesigns the end-to-end system, including data, APIs, failure and operationsPlaces the system among others: ownership boundaries, migration path, platform reuse
AmbiguityWaits for requirements or asks many unfocused questionsAsks the few questions that change the design, assumes the rest out loudReframes the problem: "the real requirement is X, so Y is out of scope"
DepthCorrect at the box levelImplementation depth in 1 to 2 areas (schema, algorithm, protocol)Depth where the risk is, plus second-order effects (cost, team load, evolution)
Trade-offsNames one optionCompares 2 options with gain, cost and the condition that would flip the choiceTies trade-offs to business and organisational constraints; knows when to defer a decision
DrivingFollows the interviewerDrives the agenda, checkpoints, manages timeDrives and adapts; treats the interviewer as a stakeholder
Failure and opsMentions replicationDetection, blast radius, degradation, recovery, rolloutSLOs, on-call cost, incident history, safe evolution of schemas and APIs

Senior signal: you finish a decision without being asked "why?". The "why", the cost and the alternative come in the same sentence as the choice.

How feedback becomes a decision

How senior loops work

A common process: each interviewer writes notes against a rubric and a hire / no-hire recommendation with a level, then a debrief or hiring committee reads them together. Evidence in the notes counts; impressions do not.

  • Consistency matters: four "lean hire at senior" often beats two "strong hire" plus two "no hire".
  • Down-levelling is a common outcome: a pass at senior with staff-level coding but mid-level design scope.
  • Make evidence easy to write down: say the decision, the reason and the cost as one sentence the interviewer can quote.

What interviewers score

What interviewers score

Senior signal: operational maturity is where nine years of experience shows most cheaply. One sentence on how you would roll it out and what alert would wake you up is often more convincing than another component.

Where each signal is earned

What interviewers score

  • If the interviewer takes over a phase (for example "skip estimates"), that dimension is not lost; say your assumed scale in one sentence and move on.
  • Plan to reach failures and scale with at least 5 minutes left; it is the dimension most often left blank.

Driving: assume, checkpoint, propose, deepen

Running the session

Driving means the interviewer never has to ask "what next?". Use the same small loop at every step: state an assumption, propose a concrete option, check in, then deepen or switch.

  • Assume out loud: "I'll assume 10M DAU and that a few seconds of staleness on counts is fine."
  • Signpost: "Three things to cover: the write path, the read path, then the hot-key problem."
  • Checkpoint briefly: "Does that match what you had in mind?" every 5 to 8 minutes, not after every sentence.
  • Propose, do not ask open questions: "I'd use X. Shall I go on?" beats "What database should I use?".
  • Take hints: a question like "how would this behave if one user had 50M followers?" is a direction, not small talk.

Trade-off phrasing templates

Running the session

The core template: "I'd choose X because ..., the cost is ..., I'd revisit if ...". The third clause is what separates senior from mid-level: it shows you know the boundary of your own decision.

SituationTemplateExample
Making a choiceI'd choose X because R; the cost is C; I'd revisit if T."Fan-out on write, because reads outnumber writes 100:1; the cost is write amplification for big accounts; I'd revisit for users above about 100k followers."
Rejecting an optionY would also work, but it pays C for a benefit we do not need."Multi-leader would give local writes in each region, but we have no regional write requirement, and conflicts are expensive."
Starting simpleStart with X; switch to Y when metric M passes N."One Postgres primary; shard by user_id when writes pass about 5k/s or data passes a few TB."
UncertaintyIt depends on A; if A, then X; otherwise Y. Which is closer?"If balances must never be stale, a leader read; if seconds are fine, a replica. Which do you need?"
Per-operation splitFor operation 1, X; for operation 2, Y."Seat reservation is strongly consistent; seat-map browsing is cached and may lag a few seconds."
Accepting pushbackGood point: that breaks assumption A. Then I'd change to Y, which costs C."Right, ordering per chat matters; I'd partition the stream by chat_id."
Holding a positionI considered that; I still prefer X because R, unless T."I still prefer a queue here because consumers do not need replay, unless we add analytics consumers."

Interview tip: avoid "it depends" as an ending. It is fine as a beginning only if the next words name what it depends on and what you would pick for each case.

"What if this fails?"

Running the session

Answer in a fixed order so nothing is missed: how it is detected, what the blast radius is, what users see, how it recovers, whether data is lost, and how recurrence is prevented. Mechanisms are in Reliability and Operations.

Component lostA strong first answer
Cache clusterDatabase sees the full read load: protect it with request coalescing, rate limits and a warm-up path; serve stale where allowed
Primary databasePromote a replica (seconds to a minute); async replication may lose the last writes; writes fail fast meanwhile; idempotency keys make client retries safe
Queue or brokerProducers buffer or fail fast; replicated broker keeps acknowledged messages; consumers are idempotent so redelivery is safe
Downstream dependencyTimeout, bounded retries with jitter, circuit breaker, fallback response; never let it exhaust your threads
Whole availability zoneInstances spread over 3 AZs with headroom to lose one; quorum stores keep a majority
A bad deployCanary, automatic rollback on error-rate SLO breach, feature flag to switch off

"Scale it 100x"

Running the session

Do not add boxes at random. Multiply the numbers, find the first resource to hit its limit, fix that, and repeat. Say which parts of the design do not change.

MultiplierWhat usually breaksTypical change
10xApp CPU, database reads, connection countsMore stateless instances, replicas, a cache, connection pooling
100xDatabase writes and size, hot keys, fan-out cost, single-broker throughputSharding, hot-key splitting, async fan-out, partitioned streams, precomputation
1000xOne region, cost, operational toil, global coordinationMulti-region, cell-based architecture, tiered storage, automation; avoid any global lock or counter

Senior signal: mention cost. At 100x, storage and egress bills are often the real constraint, and tiering cold data or compressing is a design change, not an afterthought.

Drawing order on the whiteboard

Running the session

Draw left to right in the order a request travels, then add the asynchronous and caching layers once the basic path works. The board then reads like the walkthrough you give.

  1. Clients and edge, services, stores: the minimum end-to-end path.
  2. Trace the write path with arrows, then the read path; label arrows with the call, not with protocol trivia.
  3. Add queues and workers for anything the user does not wait for.
  4. Add caches and replicas where your numbers said reads dominate.
  5. Annotate key numbers next to the boxes (QPS, size) and the data model next to the stores.

Interview tip: in a virtual round, use a simple tool you have practised with. Boxes and arrows drawn quickly beat a pretty diagram finished at minute 30.

When you are stuck

Running the session

Silence is the expensive part, not being stuck. Narrate and fall back to something that is known to work.

  • "Let me first do the simple version: one service and one table. Then I'll fix what breaks."
  • "I haven't used X in production; my understanding is ..., which matters here because ..." Honest and still useful.
  • If you made a mistake earlier, say so and correct it. Self-correction is a positive signal.

Flowchart: choosing a database

Running the session

Start from the access pattern and the invariants, never from the product. Comparison detail is in Data Systems.

  • Polyglot is normal: a relational source of truth plus a search index plus a cache, kept in sync through change data capture or events.
  • Say the shard or partition key in the same breath as the store: it is the choice that is hard to undo.

Flowchart: sync vs async, queue vs stream

Running the session

Synchronous only for what the caller must wait for. Behind the async boundary, a work queue distributes tasks; a log or stream keeps an ordered, replayable history for many consumers. Detail in Building Blocks and API and Communication Design.

 Work queue (RabbitMQ / SQS-like)Log / stream (Kafka-like)
Message after consumptionDeleted once acknowledgedRetained for a period; consumers track offsets
ConsumersCompeting workers share the workMany independent consumer groups each see everything
OrderingWeak or per-group (FIFO variants)Strict within a partition
Good forJobs, emails, retries with delay, dead-letteringEvent sourcing, CDC, analytics fan-out, replay after a bug

How to steer

Deep-dive menu

  • Depth checklist for any area: data model and keys, the algorithm or protocol, concurrency, the failure case, one number.
  • Prepare two "home ground" deep dives from your own experience (for example caching and idempotent payments) and practise steering towards them when they fit.

Presenting your own system

Project deep dive and behavioural

Pick a system where you made the key decisions, it ran in production, and you can discuss what went wrong. Present it in six beats, about 10 minutes, then expect 30 minutes of probing.

BeatSayTime
ContextBusiness problem, users, scale, team size, your role1 min
ConstraintsLatency, consistency, deadline, legacy, budget, team skills1 min
Your decisions2 to 3 decisions you drove, with the alternatives you rejected; use "I" for yours, "we" for the team's3 min
Trade-offsWhat each decision cost, and how you mitigated it2 min
OutcomeMeasured results: p99, cost, incidents, adoption, delivery date1 min
What you would changeOne or two honest corrections and why1 min

Interview tip: expect "why not X?" for every major decision and "what was the hardest bug or incident?". Prepare answers for both; vagueness here reads as not having owned it.

The one-diagram template

Project deep dive and behavioural

Practise drawing your system in under three minutes, in this shape: your part in the middle, its neighbours around it, and the one change you drove marked.

  • Annotate two or three numbers on it: request rate, data size, latency target.
  • Mark the component you changed and be ready to draw its internals as a second level.
  • Remove anything confidential; describe the shape, not the employer's internal names.

Final-week checklist

Study plan

  • Rounds, lengths and level confirmed with the recruiter; LLD format (diagram or running code) known.
  • Drawing tool chosen and practised; for machine coding, IDE and language set up with a project template that runs.
  • One timed HLD per day, a different family each day (question families).
  • The trade-off template and the failure sequence said out loud until automatic.
  • Project deep dive: one diagram, six beats, two "why not X" answers, one incident.
  • 8 to 10 STAR stories mapped to the themes; each under 3 minutes.
  • Key numbers from Foundations reviewed once; no new topics after two days before.
  • Questions for each interviewer prepared (team, on-call, how design decisions get made).
